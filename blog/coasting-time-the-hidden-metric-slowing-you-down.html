Coasting Time: The Hidden Metric Slowing You Down

By ThrottleX Team Published October 12, 2026 Read time 8 min

Ask riders where they lose time and they will tell you about braking, lean angle, or corner speed. Almost nobody mentions coasting. Yet coasting is one of the most common habits holding riders back on both the street and the track, and it hides in plain sight because it does not feel like a mistake. It feels like being careful.

Coasting is the time you spend neither braking nor accelerating. You are off the brakes, off the gas or barely holding it closed, and the bike is just rolling. A little of it is unavoidable. A lot of it costs you speed, stability, and grip. Here is what coasting is, why it matters, and how to find it in your ride data.

WHAT IS COASTING TIME?

Every corner has three basic phases of input. You brake to set your entry speed. You turn the bike in. You get back on the throttle and drive out. In an ideal corner those phases flow into each other. The brake comes off as the lean goes on, and the throttle picks up as soon as the bike is pointed where it needs to go.

Coasting is the gap between those phases. It is the moment after you release the brake but before you commit to the throttle, when the rider is waiting, hoping the corner works out. On a closed throttle the bike is not truly neutral either. Engine braking slows it gently and keeps weight on the front tire, so the bike rolls through the middle of the corner without being deliberately controlled by either the brakes or the throttle.

// The Key Point

Coasting is not a technique. It is the absence of one. It is the time where the rider has stopped controlling the bike's speed and has not yet started controlling its drive. Every second of it is a second where the chassis is unsettled and the rider is waiting instead of riding.

WHY COASTING COSTS YOU

It unsettles the chassis

A motorcycle is most stable when it is under a light, steady load. Under braking the weight is on the front. Under drive the weight moves rearward and the suspension settles into a stable attitude. On a closed throttle mid corner the bike sits in an in between state with the front still loaded and the rear light. The steering can feel vague, the bike can run wide, and small bumps upset it more than they should.

It wastes speed

While you coast the bike is slowly bleeding off speed through engine braking and drag. You already did your slowing down in the brake zone. Losing more speed in the middle of the corner means you exit slower than you needed to, and every bit of exit speed you give up gets carried all the way down the next straight.

It delays your drive

The longer you coast, the later you get on the throttle. A late throttle pickup often turns into an abrupt one, because the rider realizes the exit is coming and grabs a handful to make up for it. That sudden input while still leaned over is exactly the kind of move that upsets the rear tire.

It usually means the entry was wrong

Coasting is rarely the root problem. It is a symptom. Riders coast when they braked too early and arrived at the corner too slow, when they are unsure about the line, or when they are not looking far enough through the corner to know where the exit is. Fix the cause and the coasting goes away on its own. That is why it pairs so closely with the habit covered in Why Most Riders Brake Too Early and Too Hard.

HOW TO FIND COASTING IN YOUR DATA

Your phone cannot see your throttle grip or your brake lever. What it can see is the result of those inputs, because every brake and throttle action shows up as longitudinal G force, the forward and backward acceleration of the bike. That makes coasting easy to spot once you know what to look for.

In the G force trace

Look at the longitudinal G across a corner. Braking shows up as a clear negative spike. Drive shows up as positive G building on the exit. Coasting is the flat stretch in between, where the trace sits slightly below zero from engine braking and does almost nothing. The longer that flat section, the more time you spent waiting. Compare the same corner across several laps or several rides and you will see whether that gap is shrinking.

On the friction circle

The ThrottleX friction circle plots lateral G against longitudinal G. A rider with good flow traces a smooth arc around the outside of the circle, moving from braking into cornering into drive. A rider who coasts shows a cluster of points pushed out to the left and right sides with almost no longitudinal G, then a sharp jump toward drive. That sideways cluster is the coasting phase made visible.

Alongside your lean angle

Match the coasting stretch against your lean angle. If the flat section of the G trace sits right where you reach peak lean, you are rolling through the apex with nothing going on. If it starts well before you even turn in, you braked too early and are rolling toward the corner. Those are two different problems with two different fixes.

HOW TO REDUCE COASTING

Release the brakes into the turn in

Instead of finishing all your braking upright and then rolling into the corner, ease off the brakes progressively as you start to lean. The braking force fades out at the same rate the lean comes in. This is the foundation of trail braking, and even a gentle version of it closes most of the gap on corner entry. Our guide to trail braking walks through how to build it safely.

Get to maintenance throttle early

As soon as the bike is turned in and you are happy with your line, crack the throttle open just enough to hold a steady speed. This is maintenance throttle. It settles the suspension and transfers a little weight rearward, which makes the bike feel more planted. From there, roll the throttle on smoothly and steadily as you stand the bike up for the exit.

Look further ahead

Much of coasting is hesitation, and hesitation comes from not knowing what the corner does next. Moving your eyes to the exit early gives you the information to commit to the throttle sooner. If you have not worked on your vision deliberately, start with The Right Way to Look Through a Corner.

Pick one corner and work it

Do not try to eliminate coasting everywhere at once. Pick one corner you ride often, check how long the flat section in your G trace lasts, and set a goal to shorten it next session. When that corner improves, move to the next one. Our post on using ride data to set goals covers how to structure that.

// A Word of Caution

Shrinking your coasting time does not mean braking later and harder everywhere or getting on the gas while you are still unsure. On the street, a bit of coasting into a blind corner is a sensible safety margin. The goal is to remove the hesitation that comes from poor technique, not the buffer that comes from good judgment.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Coasting is the quiet time in a corner where nothing is being controlled. It bleeds speed, unsettles the bike, and almost always points back to an entry problem like braking too early, choosing an unsure line, or looking too close. It never feels like a mistake, which is exactly why so few riders work on it.

Your data does not have that blind spot. Find the flat stretch in your G force trace, find the sideways cluster on your friction circle, and work on shrinking them one corner at a time. Smooth riders are not braking harder or leaning further. They are simply spending less time waiting.
