How Your Phone Measures Lean Angle

By ThrottleX Team Published October 30, 2026 Read time 9 min

Every rider who sees a lean angle number on a phone asks the same question sooner or later. Can a phone clamped to the bars really know how far the bike is leaned over? It is a fair question. The answer is yes, but not in the way most people assume, and the reason why is one of the more interesting pieces of physics in motorcycling.

The short version is that no single sensor in your phone can measure lean angle on its own. The accelerometer gets fooled in every corner. The gyroscope drifts over time. Getting a trustworthy number means combining them, and that process is called sensor fusion. Here is how each sensor works, where each one fails, and how fusing them gives you a lean angle you can actually use.

THE TWO SENSORS THAT DO THE WORK

Every modern smartphone carries an inertial measurement unit, usually called the IMU. It is a tiny chip that contains two key sensors, each measuring along three axes.

  • The accelerometer measures acceleration. When the phone sits still, the only thing it feels is gravity pulling straight down, so it can tell which way is down. That is how your screen knows to rotate when you turn the phone sideways.
  • The gyroscope measures rotation rate. It does not know what angle the phone is at. It knows how fast the phone is turning, in degrees per second, around each of its three axes.

On paper either sensor looks like it should be able to measure lean. In practice each one has a flaw that makes it unusable on its own once the bike is actually moving.

WHY THE ACCELEROMETER GETS FOOLED IN A CORNER

Park your bike on the side stand and tilt your phone with it. The accelerometer sees gravity pulling down at an angle relative to the phone, and the math to turn that into a lean angle is simple. This is how a level app works, and on a parked bike it works fine.

Now ride through a corner. Two forces are acting on the bike at the same time. Gravity pulls straight down. The centripetal acceleration of the turn pulls toward the inside of the corner. The bike leans precisely so that the combination of those two forces passes through the line from the tire contact patch up through the center of mass. That balance is the whole reason a motorcycle can lean into a corner without falling over.

Here is the problem. The accelerometer feels that combined force, not gravity alone. And because the bike is leaned exactly so the combined force lines up with the bike, the accelerometer sees the force pointing straight down through the chassis. From the phone's point of view the bike looks almost perfectly upright, just heavier than normal.

// The Key Point

In a steady corner, an accelerometer on its own will read close to zero degrees of lean even when the bike is at 40 degrees. It does not see the lean. It sees a stronger pull straight down through the bike. That is why any app that calculates lean from the accelerometer alone, the way a level app does, gives you numbers that are badly wrong on the road.

The accelerometer is not useless in a corner. That extra pull it feels is real information. At 45 degrees of lean the bike carries roughly 1.4 times its normal weight through the chassis, and that load factor is directly tied to the lean. But the accelerometer cannot tell you the angle by pointing at it.

WHY THE GYROSCOPE DRIFTS

The gyroscope is not fooled by cornering forces at all. It measures how fast the bike is rolling. When you tip into a corner at 40 degrees per second for one second, the gyroscope sees exactly that. Add up every tiny slice of roll rate over time, a process called integration, and you get the lean angle.

This works beautifully over a few seconds. Over a few minutes it falls apart. Every gyroscope has a tiny built in error called bias. Even when the phone is perfectly still the gyroscope might report a fraction of a degree per second of rotation that is not really happening. Integration adds that error up every single sample. A bias of a tenth of a degree per second becomes six degrees of false lean after one minute, and it keeps growing. Ride for twenty minutes on gyroscope integration alone and the bike would appear to be lying on its side at a stop light.

Temperature makes this worse. Bias shifts as the phone warms up in the sun or cools down at speed, so it cannot be measured once and subtracted forever.

SENSOR FUSION: USING EACH SENSOR WHERE IT IS STRONG

The two sensors fail in opposite ways, and that is exactly what makes combining them work.

  • The gyroscope is accurate in the short term and drifts in the long term. It nails the fast changes, like your turn in and the transition from one corner to the next.
  • The accelerometer is noisy and fooled in the short term but it never drifts. On straights and at stops, when the bike is upright and not cornering, it gives a solid reference for where true vertical is.

Sensor fusion lets the gyroscope carry the lean angle through the fast action of a corner, then uses every moment where the accelerometer can be trusted to quietly pull the estimate back toward the truth and cancel out the drift. The result tracks quick movements like the gyroscope and holds its zero like the accelerometer.

The Kalman filter

ThrottleX does this fusion with a Kalman filter. A Kalman filter keeps a running model of how the bike should behave and an estimate of how much it trusts each sensor at every moment. Each new sample is compared against what the model predicted. If the gyroscope and accelerometer agree, the estimate firms up. If they disagree, the filter weighs each one by how reliable it is in that situation, and it can even estimate the gyroscope bias itself and remove it as you ride. It is the same family of algorithm used in aircraft navigation, and it is far more capable than a simple average of the two sensors.

Why sample rate matters

The filter is only as good as the data it is fed. A fast flick from full lean on one side to full lean on the other can happen in well under a second. At 20 samples per second the filter gets a handful of snapshots of that transition. At 100 samples per second it gets five times as many, which means sharper peaks, less smoothing of real events, and a cleaner Flick Rate. ThrottleX runs the IMU at up to 100Hz on supported hardware for exactly this reason.

WHAT STILL AFFECTS YOUR NUMBER

Tire width

The physics above assumes a tire with no width, a perfect knife edge. Real motorcycle tires are wide and round. As you lean, the contact patch rolls out toward the edge of the tire and away from the bike's centerline. To balance the same corner, a bike on wide tires has to lean a few degrees further than the theory says. Different bikes carry different tire widths and different centers of mass, which is why ThrottleX asks you to set your bike geometry as Sport, Cruiser, or Touring. That setting tells the fusion what kind of machine it is working with.

Your mount

The IMU measures the phone, not the bike. If the phone flexes, buzzes, or shifts on its mount, the sensors record that movement as if the whole bike did it. Engine vibration adds noise to both sensors, and a mount that slowly rotates will shift your zero point. A rigid mount with a vibration damper is the single biggest upgrade you can make to your data. We cover that in detail in How to Mount Your Phone on a Motorcycle for Telemetry.

Calibration

No mount holds a phone perfectly level to the bike. Calibrating with the bike upright tells the app what zero degrees looks like for your specific mount angle. Skip it and every reading carries an offset. Recalibrate any time you move the mount, change phones, or knock the mount out of position.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Your phone does not measure lean angle directly, and neither does a dedicated data logger. Both estimate it by fusing a gyroscope that is quick but drifts with an accelerometer that is steady but gets fooled in corners. The quality of the number comes down to how well that fusion is done, how fast the sensors are sampled, and how solidly the phone is mounted to the bike.

Get those three things right and the phone in your pocket produces lean data that a few years ago required a dedicated logger costing thousands. Get any of them wrong and no amount of clever math will save the number. Mount it solid, calibrate it upright, and let the filter do its job.
